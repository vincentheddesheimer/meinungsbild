\section{Validation design}

The production MRP model uses federal election results (AfD vote share, CDU/CSU vote share, turnout) as county-level covariates. Including actual vote shares on the right-hand side when validating predicted vote shares would be partly circular. The validation models therefore exclude all election covariates, using only individual demographics, county-level socioeconomic covariates, and geographic random effects. This makes the comparison a genuine out-of-sample test: the model must recover county-level partisan variation from survey responses and geographic structure alone.

\section{Data}

\paragraph{Ground truth.} Official BTW 2021 county-level second-vote (Zweitstimme) shares for six parties (CDU/CSU, SPD, Gr\"une, FDP, AfD, DIE LINKE) across all 400 Kreise, from the German Election Database (GERDA).

\paragraph{Survey data.} GLES Tracking and Cross-Section Cumulation respondents with valid vote intention. We test three year windows: 2021 only (4,954 respondents; 4,533 observations per party model after dropping missing demographics), 2020--2021 (7,704 respondents; 7,097 per model), and 2019--2021 (10,411 respondents; 9,642 per model). All windows cover 245 Kreise with at least one respondent. Counties without survey respondents receive predictions via the demographic cell structure, state-level random effects, and fixed effects, without county-specific intercepts.

\paragraph{Poststratification.} Zensus 2022 cross-tabulations of age (5 groups), gender (2), education (5 levels), and employment status (2) at the Kreis level, yielding 100 demographic cells per county (40,000 cells total).

\section{Model}

Each validation model is a logistic multilevel regression estimated with \texttt{lme4::glmer()}:
%
\begin{align*}
y_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\pi_i), \quad \text{logit}(\pi_i) &= \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \sum_k \alpha^{(k)}_{g_k(i)}
\end{align*}
%
where $\mathbf{x}_i$ contains individual-level covariates (gender, employment status) and county-level covariates (log population density, plus specification-dependent socioeconomic variables). The random effects $\alpha^{(k)}$ include varying intercepts for age (5), education (5), three pairwise demographic interactions (education $\times$ age, gender $\times$ age, gender $\times$ education), an employment $\times$ age interaction (in employment specifications only), survey source, legislative period, and cross-classified geographic intercepts for state (16), county (~245 with respondents), and electoral district (~299). Counties and electoral districts are cross-classified rather than nested, since Wahlkreis boundaries do not align with county boundaries. The model is estimated with \texttt{nAGQ = 0} (Laplace approximation) and the BOBYQA optimizer. If the full model fails to converge, the electoral district random effect is dropped and the model is re-estimated.

Baseline specifications use a 50-cell poststratification frame (5 age $\times$ 2 gender $\times$ 5 education); employment specifications expand this to 100 cells by adding the employment dimension. County-level estimates are obtained by poststratifying predicted probabilities over these demographic cells, weighted by Zensus population counts.

\section{Covariate selection}

Rather than selecting covariates by penalizing the first-stage model (L1/LASSO on individual-level BIC), we use greedy forward selection on the end-to-end validation target. Starting from a baseline with log population density only, we add one county-level covariate at a time, run the full MRP pipeline (fit, predict, poststratify), and retain the covariate that most improves the median correlation across the six parties. The procedure stops when no candidate improves the median $r$.

The candidate covariates (all from INKAR via the \texttt{gerda} R package, z-scored) are: share of foreign population, median income, unemployment rate, share aged 65+, GDP per capita, share of school leavers with Abitur, purchasing power, and secondary sector share of gross value added.

\subsection{Cross-validation}

Forward selection on the full set of 400 counties risks overfitting to the specific geographic distribution of BTW 2021 results. To obtain an honest out-of-sample estimate, we run 5-fold spatial cross-validation: the 400 counties are randomly split into 5 folds of 80, and in each fold the forward selection procedure uses only the 320 training counties to evaluate candidate covariates. The selected covariates are then evaluated on the 80 held-out counties. Each county appears in exactly one test fold, so the combined test-set performance covers all 400 counties without data leakage in the selection step.

Table~\ref{tab:cv} reports the results. The mean train-set median $r$ across folds is 0.853, while the mean test-set median $r$ is 0.826 (SD = 0.052). The gap of 0.027 indicates moderate overfitting in the non-CV forward selection estimate of 0.857. The honest CV estimate of 0.831 (computed from the combined test-set predictions across all folds) is only marginally above the baseline without covariates (0.829), suggesting that county-level socioeconomic covariates provide limited additional predictive power beyond what the geographic random effects already capture.

\section{Key findings}

Five results stand out. First, more survey data consistently improves predictions: the 2019--2021 window (9,642 observations) outperforms the 2021-only window (4,533) regardless of model specification. Second, covariate selection must optimize the final validation target. Entering all 8 INKAR covariates without selection degrades county-level performance (median $r$ = 0.696, vs.\ 0.829 for the covariate-free baseline with the same data). Adding covariates that improve individual-level fit can compete with the county random effect for geographic variance, reducing the model's ability to differentiate counties after poststratification. Forward selection on the end-to-end validation target avoids this by retaining only covariates that improve poststratified county-level predictions. Third, 5-fold spatial cross-validation reveals that much of the forward selection gain is overfitting: the honest out-of-sample estimate (median $r$ = 0.831) is only marginally above the covariate-free baseline (0.829). Unemployment rate is the only covariate selected in all 5 folds; most other covariates are unstable across geographic subsets. The most defensible production model would add at most unemployment rate and share aged 65+. Fourth, letting demographic effects vary by state improves predictions slightly. Adding an age-by-state random effect ($1 | \text{age\_cat:state\_code}$, 80 cells) raises median $r$ from 0.829 to 0.832, the largest structural gain we found. Education-by-state (0.830) and gender-by-state (0.830) add less, and combining multiple demographic-by-state interactions does not improve over age-by-state alone. The age-by-state interaction captures systematic regional differences in how age relates to party preference (e.g., the age gradient of AfD support differs between East and West). Fifth, adding employment as an individual-level demographic provides a modest improvement (0.824 to 0.826 for the 2020--2021 baseline).

We also tested temporal weighting to downweight older observations within the 2019--2021 window, assigning kernel weights that decay with distance from 2021 (e.g., $w_{2019} = 0.33$, $w_{2020} = 0.67$, $w_{2021} = 1.0$ for linear decay). All weighting schemes performed equal to or worse than uniform weights (median $r$: 0.830 for mild decay, 0.826 for linear, 0.820 for steep, vs.\ 0.829 for equal weights). The legislative period random effect already absorbs systematic temporal shifts, and downweighting reduces the effective sample size without a compensating gain in temporal relevance.

For the vote share validation, the current specification is likely near its ceiling. The main portable gains are more survey data (2019--2021 vs.\ 2021 only) and the age-by-state interaction. Further expanding generic interaction structure (e.g., education $\times$ age $\times$ state) is unlikely to move performance much given the data constraints. For policy issues beyond vote choice, the most useful omitted structure may be issue-specific rather than generic: whether the education effect, urban-rural gradient, or East-West divide matters for a particular item.

FDP is the weakest-performing party ($r$ = 0.573), likely because its vote share has low geographic variance (SD = 2.2 percentage points across counties, the lowest of the six parties) and its supporters are not well predicted by standard demographics. All other parties exceed $r$ = 0.82. The validation is conservative: the production model includes election covariates (excluded here), so production estimates should perform better than these numbers suggest.
