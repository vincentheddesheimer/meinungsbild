\section{Validation design}

The production MRP model includes federal election results (AfD vote share, CDU/CSU vote share, turnout) as county-level covariates. Using actual vote shares to predict vote shares would be partly circular. The validation models therefore exclude all election covariates, using only individual demographics, county-level socioeconomic covariates, and geographic random effects. The model must recover county-level partisan variation from survey responses and geographic structure alone.

\section{Data}

\paragraph{Ground truth.} Official BTW 2021 county-level second-vote (Zweitstimme) shares for six parties (CDU/CSU, SPD, Gr\"une, FDP, AfD, DIE LINKE) across all 400 Kreise, from the German Election Database (GERDA).

\paragraph{Survey data.} GLES Tracking and Cross-Section Cumulation respondents with valid vote intention, pooled across 2019--2021 (10,411 respondents; 9,642 observations per party model after dropping missing demographics). The survey covers 245 of 400 Kreise with at least one respondent. Counties without respondents receive predictions through the model's partial pooling and the poststratification structure (Section 3), without county-specific intercepts.

\paragraph{Poststratification.} Zensus 2022 cross-tabulations of age (5 groups), gender (2), education (5 levels), and employment status (2) at the Kreis level. Specifications without employment use a 50-cell frame (5 age $\times$ 2 gender $\times$ 5 education); specifications with employment expand to 100 cells per county.

\section{Model}

Each specification fits a logistic multilevel regression:
%
\begin{align*}
y_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\pi_i), \quad \text{logit}(\pi_i) &= \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol{\beta} + \sum_k \alpha^{(k)}_{g_k(i)}
\end{align*}
%
where $g_k(i)$ indexes the group membership of respondent $i$ along random-effect dimension $k$, $\mathbf{x}_i$ contains individual-level fixed effects (gender, and optionally employment status; see Section 4) and county-level covariates (log population density, plus specification-dependent socioeconomic variables). The random effects $\alpha^{(k)}$ include varying intercepts for age (5 levels), education (5), three pairwise demographic interactions (education $\times$ age, gender $\times$ age, gender $\times$ education), survey source, legislative period, and geographic intercepts for state (16), county (~245 with respondents), and electoral district (~299). Counties and electoral districts are cross-classified rather than nested, since Wahlkreis boundaries do not align with county boundaries. County-level estimates are obtained by poststratifying predicted probabilities over the demographic cells, weighted by Zensus population counts.

Models are estimated with the glmmTMB package (Laplace approximation). If the full model fails to converge, the electoral district random effect is dropped and the model is re-estimated.

\section{Parameter space}

Three specification choices lack clear theoretical guidance: state-varying demographic slopes, county-level covariates, and individual-level employment status. We search over a grid of all three, holding the year window fixed at 2019--2021 (which outperforms narrower windows in preliminary tests).

\paragraph{State-varying demographic slopes.} The baseline model assumes that the relationship between demographics and vote choice is the same in every state. Allowing age or education effects to vary by state (via additional random intercepts for age $\times$ state or education $\times$ state) can capture systematic regional differences, such as the age gradient of AfD support differing between East and West. We test four options: no state-varying slopes, age $\times$ state (80 levels), education $\times$ state (80 levels), or both (160 levels).

\paragraph{County-level covariates.} Beyond log population density (included in all specifications), county-level socioeconomic covariates can help the model predict vote shares in counties with few or no survey respondents. Adding too many covariates risks overfitting, particularly with only 245 observed counties. From the INKAR database (via the GERDA R package), we consider four covariate pairs alongside the no-covariate baseline: unemployment rate alone, unemployment + share of school leavers with Abitur, unemployment + foreign-born share, and unemployment + secondary sector share of gross value added. All covariates are z-scored.

\paragraph{Employment status.} Employment status (binary: employed or not) can be included as an individual-level predictor. We test three options: no employment variable, employment as a fixed effect only (using the 100-cell poststratification frame), or employment as a fixed effect plus an employment $\times$ age random effect interaction.

The full grid has $4 \times 5 \times 3 = 60$ specifications.

\section{Cross-validation procedure}

We evaluate all 60 specifications via 5-fold spatial cross-validation. The 400 ground-truth counties are randomly split into 5 folds of 80. For each specification and each fold, we fit the model on all survey data, poststratify to all 400 counties, and compute the median Pearson correlation ($r$) across the six parties separately on the 320 selection counties and the 80 held-out test counties. Respondents in held-out counties still contribute to the model fit; the held-out distinction applies only to the evaluation metric. This tests whether a specification generalizes geographically, not whether the model can predict from unseen individuals. Each county appears in exactly one test fold, so the combined test-set performance covers all 400 counties without data leakage in the specification choice.

For each specification, we report the mean test-set median $r$ across the 5 folds, along with its standard deviation. The total number of model fits is $60 \times 5 \times 6 = 1{,}800$.

County-level covariates account for most of the improvement. All top 10 specifications include unemployment rate; adding secondary sector share or Abitur share provides a further gain. Specifications without county covariates (ranks 49--60) cluster around test $r$ = 0.84, while those with unemployment and a second covariate reach 0.85. State-varying demographic slopes add at most 0.001 to test $r$: the top-ranked specification uses both age $\times$ state and education $\times$ state, but rank 5 (no state slopes, same covariates) is only 0.001 lower. Employment as a fixed effect provides a consistent improvement of 0.001--0.002, while the additional employment $\times$ age random effect does not improve over the fixed effect alone.

The difference between selection-set and test-set $r$ is small (0.002--0.004 in absolute value) for all specifications. The standard deviation across folds is lower for covariate-including specifications (~0.019) than for the baseline (~0.024), indicating that covariates stabilize predictions across geographic subsets.

FDP is the weakest-performing party across all specifications (SD = 2.2 percentage points across counties, the lowest geographic variance of the six parties). All other parties exceed $r$ = 0.82 in the baseline 2019--2021 specification.

These numbers are conservative lower bounds: the production model adds election covariates, which were excluded here.
